Application-Centric Considerations for Deployment over the IoT Computing
                               Continuum
                 draft-kamienski-iot-deployment-latest

Abstract

   IoT applications distribute sensing, processing, and actuation
   functions across constrained devices, edge nodes, and cloud
   platforms.  Deployment decisions in this computing continuum affect
   not only performance but also the ability to preserve essential
   physical behavior during connectivity loss, resource exhaustion, and
   software changes.  This document examines the application-layer
   problem of decomposing, placing, coordinating, and managing these
   functions over their lifetime.  It complements the edge challenges
   and functions described in RFC 9556 by focusing on application
   dependencies, device constraints, local autonomy, and lifecycle
   consistency.  Smart irrigation and drone delivery illustrate the
   problem space.  The document relates these concerns to existing IETF,
   IRTF, and W3C work and identifies open research questions without
   specifying protocols or a reference architecture.

1.  Introduction

   The Internet of Things (IoT) has evolved from simple data collection
   systems into a foundation for complex, data-driven smart applications
   spanning domains such as smart cities, precision agriculture,
   industrial automation and healthcare.  These applications
   increasingly depend on distributed computing resources to sense,
   process, and act on data close to where it is generated, while also
   leveraging large-scale cloud infrastructures for coordination,
   analytics, and long-term storage.

   This evolution has led to the emergence of a distributed computing
   environment spanning multiple layers, from highly constrained devices
   and gateways to edge nodes, cloudlets, and centralized cloud
   platforms.  In the literature, this environment is commonly described
   as the edge-cloud continuum, IoT-edge-cloud continuum, or simply
   computing continuum.  Comprehensive perspectives on the evolution of
   this paradigm are provided in [CONTINUUM-SURVEY].  In this document,
   the term IoT computing continuum emphasizes the presence of
   constrained IoT devices and the domain-specific requirements they
   introduce.

   Recent research and standardization efforts have highlighted the
   importance of this continuum and discussed challenges related to edge
   functions, data handling, and communication across heterogeneous
   environments, including work within the IETF and IRTF, such as
   [RFC9556].  However, much of the existing discussion remains
   infrastructure- or communication-centric, with comparatively less
   focus on the implications for smart application deployment and
   lifecycle management.

   From an application perspective, smart services are no longer
   confined to a single execution environment.  Instead, they are
   composed of multiple functions that must be placed, coordinated, and
   potentially migrated across different stages of the IoT computing
   continuum to satisfy requirements for latency, resilience,
   scalability, privacy, and energy efficiency.  In practice, these
   decisions are often handled manually or through ad hoc mechanisms,
   leading to fragmented solutions and limited portability across
   deployments.

   The lack of a shared, application-centric view of deployment over the
   IoT computing continuum creates challenges for both researchers and
   practitioners.  It complicates systematic reasoning about application
   structure, hinders the comparison of alternative approaches, and
   makes it difficult to identify common requirements that could inform
   future research and standardization activities.

   The central IoT concern is preserving application behavior at the
   physical boundary.  Sensor and actuator access anchors some functions
   to particular devices; limited energy and memory restrict feasible
   placements; and a cloud outage can interrupt a control dependency
   even when local communication remains available.  Function graphs and
   dynamic placement are general continuum concepts.  Their treatment
   here is motivated by these combined IoT constraints, rather than by a
   claim that they are unique to IoT.

   This document aims to contribute to this discussion by examining
   smart application deployment over the IoT computing continuum from a
   conceptual and architectural perspective.  Rather than proposing
   specific mechanisms or protocols, it focuses on identifying key
   challenges, clarifying terminology, and outlining considerations
   relevant to ongoing research within the Thing-to-Thing Research Group
   (T2TRG) of the Internet Research Task Force (IRTF).

2.  Scope and Non-Goals

   This document focuses on conceptual and architectural considerations
   for deploying smart applications across the IoT computing continuum.
   Its scope is limited to discussing challenges, common patterns, and
   terminology arising from distributed application logic across
   heterogeneous computing environments, including constrained IoT
   devices, edge nodes, and cloud platforms.  The intent is to support
   research-oriented discussion and to provide a shared frame of
   reference for ongoing and future work within the IRTF.  The
   discussion explicitly considers the interplay between application
   development and deployment, while remaining agnostic to specific
   tools, workflows, or operational practices.

   The document does not specify concrete deployment mechanisms,
   protocols, or interfaces.  It does not define a reference
   architecture, nor does it mandate how applications should be
   structured, deployed, or managed in practice.  Instead, it aims to
   describe the problem space and highlight issues relevant across
   multiple technologies, deployment models, and research efforts.

   In particular, the following items are explicitly outside the scope
   of this document:

   *  The specification of new communication protocols, APIs, or data
      models.

   *  The definition of normative requirements, including the use of
      requirement keywords such as MUST, SHOULD, or MAY.

   *  The standardization of specific platforms, frameworks, or
      programming models for IoT application deployment.

   *  Comprehensive performance evaluations or benchmarking of
      alternative solutions.  A limited empirical example illustrates
      placement trade-offs without prescribing a solution.

   By clearly delineating its scope and non-goals, this document seeks
   to complement existing and ongoing efforts in the computing
   continuum, including work on communication, edge functions, and
   operational aspects, while maintaining a technology-agnostic and
   application-centric perspective.

3.  Terminology

   This document uses the following terms with the meanings indicated
   below.  The definitions are intended to clarify usage in this
   document and are not meant to redefine existing terminology used
   elsewhere.

   *Internet of Things (IoT):* A system paradigm involving
   interconnected physical objects equipped with sensing, actuation,
   processing, and communication capabilities, operating under resource
   constraints, as discussed in [RFC7452].

   *Constrained Devices:* Devices with limited processing power, memory,
   energy availability, or communication capabilities.  The use of the
   term _constrained_ in this document follows the terminology defined
   in [I-D.ietf-iotops-7228bis].

   *IoT Computing Continuum:* A distributed computing environment
   spanning constrained IoT devices, edge nodes, and cloud platforms,
   where application components may execute at different stages
   depending on requirements and context.  The shorthand term _IoTinuum_
   has been used in related work to denote this same concept [IOTINUUM].
   This document uses the expanded form throughout for clarity and
   alignment with terminology used in related standardization efforts.

   *Edge Node:* An intermediate computing node located between
   constrained devices and centralized cloud infrastructures, providing
   computation, storage, and networking capabilities closer to data
   sources, as described in [RFC9556].

   *Orchestration and Choreography:* Two coordination styles for
   distributed applications.  Orchestration refers to coordination
   driven by explicit control logic, while choreography relies on
   decentralized interactions among components without a central
   coordinator.

4.  Background: The IoT Computing Continuum

   The increasing distribution of computation across networked systems
   has led to the emergence of what is commonly described as a computing
   continuum, spanning centralized cloud infrastructures, intermediate
   edge and fog nodes, and highly constrained end devices
   [CONTINUUM-SURVEY].  This paradigm reflects a shift away from
   strictly centralized processing models toward more distributed
   execution, driven by requirements related to latency, bandwidth
   efficiency, resilience, data locality, and regulatory constraints.

   Within the context of the Internet of Things (IoT), this continuum
   acquires additional characteristics that distinguish it from more
   general edge-cloud computing environments.  IoT deployments involve
   large numbers of heterogeneous devices with severe resource
   constraints, intermittent connectivity, and close interaction with
   the physical world through sensing and actuation [RFC7452].  As a
   result, computation is often pushed closer to data sources not only
   for performance reasons, but also to enable timely control actions,
   reduce energy consumption, and preserve privacy.

   Recent research and standardization activities have explored
   different aspects of this continuum.  In particular, work within the
   IETF and IRTF has highlighted the importance of edge computing and
   has discussed functional components and challenges associated with
   operating across distributed environments [RFC9556].  Existing
   documents have examined edge functions for data processing, storage,
   and communication, as well as the roles of gateways and intermediate
   nodes in bridging constrained devices to cloud services.  These
   efforts provide an important foundation for understanding the
   structural and operational properties of the continuum.

   The perspective taken in this document is complementary rather than
   competing.  While [RFC9556] characterizes functional components and
   challenges at the infrastructure level, focusing on edge functions
   and the communication fabric that supports them, the present document
   shifts attention to the application layer that operates on top of
   such infrastructure.  Specifically, it considers how smart
   applications, composed of multiple interdependent functions, are
   decomposed, placed, coordinated, and managed across the heterogeneous
   stages enabled by that infrastructure.  Application-centric concerns
   such as functional decomposition, placement, coordination style, and
   lifecycle management are largely orthogonal to the infrastructure
   view and remain comparatively underexplored in current
   standardization discussions.

   This document adopts the term _IoT computing continuum_ to refer to
   this distributed execution environment, emphasizing the integration
   of constrained IoT devices, edge nodes, and cloud platforms into a
   unified computational space.  Building on existing work, the focus
   here is on understanding how the properties of the IoT computing
   continuum influence application deployment, placement, and lifecycle
   management, rather than on defining specific technologies or
   architectural solutions.

5.  An Application-Centric View of the Continuum

   An application can be described as a graph of functions connected by
   data and control dependencies.  Separating this logical graph from
   its mapping onto devices, edge nodes, and cloud platforms makes
   deployment choices explicit.  This abstraction does not imply that
   every function is portable: direct sensor or actuator access, runtime
   support, available memory, and trust relationships may anchor
   functions to particular environments.

   The graph captures more than data processing.  For an irrigation
   application, it includes measurement acquisition, validation,
   decision support, authorization, and actuation.  Dependencies have
   operational meaning: a local valve controller may need a valid
   setpoint even when remote optimization is unreachable.  Describing
   the graph therefore involves identifying the behavior that remains
   available when only part of the application can communicate.

   Deployment maps functions to execution environments and establishes
   their interactions.  It includes initial installation as well as
   configuration, update, migration, recovery, and retirement.
   Coordination may use orchestration, choreography, or a combination.
   Neither style by itself guarantees continuity; the relevant question
   is which dependencies and decision authorities remain usable under
   the expected failure conditions.

6.  IoT-Specific Deployment Challenges

   Several concerns also arise in general distributed computing.  The
   following combinations make them particularly important for IoT
   deployment.

6.1.  Physical Coupling and Local Autonomy

   Functions that access sensors and actuators cannot be moved
   independently of the physical interfaces they use.  Moving a decision
   function can lengthen or interrupt a control dependency.  A
   deployment description needs to distinguish functions that may be
   offloaded from behavior that remains locally available, including
   domain-specific limits on actuation and operation with stale
   observations.  These limits depend on the application; this document
   does not assume a universal control deadline.

6.2.  Resource and Energy Constraints

   Constrained devices may lack the memory, storage, energy budget, or
   runtime needed for a candidate function.  They may also be unable to
   keep both old and new software images or execute a migration protocol
   while continuing normal operation.  Logical portability therefore
   does not imply executable portability.  Deployment feasibility
   includes hardware and runtime compatibility, access to peripherals,
   and the energy and communication costs of installation and state
   transfer.

6.3.  Intermittent Connectivity and State Reconciliation

   Sleeping devices, lossy access networks, and intermittent wide-area
   links limit both application traffic and management visibility.  An
   unreachable device is not necessarily failed.  Observations used for
   placement may be stale, and an edge node may need to continue
   operating without a remote coordinator.  Reconnection raises
   questions about reconciling measurements, configuration versions,
   queued commands, and control authority.  Replaying an obsolete
   actuator command can be materially different from replaying a stored
   measurement.

6.4.  Lifecycle Consistency across Heterogeneous Components

   Firmware, gateway services, edge runtimes, and cloud software may
   have different update mechanisms and maintenance windows.  A
   successful update of one component does not establish that the
   application remains compatible as a whole.  Partial rollout,
   rollback, and migration need to account for interface versions,
   persistent state, and the effect of interruption on ongoing sensing
   and actuation.

6.5.  Administrative and Trust Boundaries

   Device owners, connectivity providers, edge operators, and
   application providers may control different parts of a deployment.  A
   resource that is technically reachable is not necessarily authorized
   to host a function or handle its data.  Placement and lifecycle
   actions therefore depend on permissions and trust as well as
   capacity.  Moving a function or its state can change the parties able
   to observe data or issue physical commands.

7.  Conceptual Deployment Model

   The following elements provide a vocabulary for describing deployment
   decisions, rather than a reference architecture or a new data model:

   *  Application description: functions, data and control dependencies,
      state, and behavior expected during partial failure.

   *  Execution context: available resources, runtime capabilities,
      physical interfaces, connectivity, ownership, and trust
      boundaries.

   *  Deployment mapping: the assignment of functions to environments,
      their communication dependencies, and the authority to change
      those assignments.

   *  Lifecycle transition: an installation, update, reconfiguration,
      migration, recovery, or retirement operation, including its
      preconditions and treatment of state.

   *  Runtime feedback: observations about resource use, reachability,
      completion, and application behavior, together with their
      freshness and uncertainty.

   A placement is feasible only if it respects both execution
   constraints and application dependencies.  For example, moving
   irrigation optimization to the cloud can exploit additional capacity
   while leaving local validation and actuation available during a WAN
   outage.  The choice also raises a lifecycle question: which
   configuration and model version govern local behavior while the
   remote function is unavailable?

   Coordination choices are evaluated against the same dependencies.
   Central orchestration can maintain a global deployment view but may
   become unreachable.  Local interactions can preserve some behavior
   during disconnection but require rules for resolving conflicting
   state or authority after reconnection.  Hybrid arrangements need to
   make these responsibilities explicit.

   Development and deployment influence one another.  A design that
   requires synchronous cloud approval for every actuation cannot gain
   local autonomy through placement alone; its dependency structure must
   change.  Conversely, introducing a local fallback creates obligations
   for state synchronization, version compatibility, and testing.
   Development and operations feedback remains useful, but release
   procedures need to account for sleeping devices, limited update
   storage, interrupted rollout, and physical operation during
   maintenance.

8.  Illustrative Scenarios

   We present two illustrative scenarios that ground the conceptual
   deployment model discussed earlier.  The scenarios are intended
   solely to demonstrate how application-centric concepts, deployment
   decisions, and coordination styles manifest in realistic settings,
   without prescribing specific technologies or architectures.  The
   first scenario describes a smart irrigation application deployed in
   operational agricultural settings, providing a concrete reference for
   placement and lifecycle concerns under real-world constraints.  The
   second scenario describes a drone-based delivery application that
   spans a broader range of continuum stages and exhibits a richer
   interplay between coordination styles.

8.1.  Smart Irrigation in Precision Agriculture

   Smart irrigation applications collect environmental data, such as
   soil moisture and weather conditions, and use it to determine how
   much, when, and where to irrigate.  In operational deployments, these
   applications typically span constrained sensor devices in the field,
   low-power gateways acting as radio concentrators, on-premises edge
   nodes located in farm facilities, and cloud services used for
   analytics, forecasting, and long-term storage.  End users interact
   with the application through mobile or web interfaces to inspect data
   and approve irrigation plans.

   From an application-centric perspective, the irrigation scenario
   exemplifies how functional decomposition maps naturally onto multiple
   continuum stages.  Sensing and local preprocessing functions execute
   on constrained devices and field gateways.  Intermediate functions,
   such as data filtering, sensor calibration, and resilience against
   disconnections, execute on on-premises edge nodes, which are
   positioned close to the irrigation infrastructure and remain
   operational even when connectivity to the cloud is unavailable.  More
   computationally intensive functions, including data fusion, soil
   moisture forecasting, and irrigation optimization, run on cloud
   platforms that offer greater resources and broader data visibility.

   A defining property of this scenario is the need to continue
   operating under intermittent wide-area connectivity, a common
   condition in rural deployments.  Functions placed at on-premises edge
   nodes are expected to sustain essential behavior when cloud services
   are temporarily unreachable, such as enforcing safety thresholds or
   executing simplified local models, and to reconcile state once
   connectivity is restored.  This requirement directly influences how
   application functions are decomposed at development time and where
   they are deployed, illustrating the bidirectional influence between
   development and deployment concerns discussed in earlier sections.

   The irrigation scenario also highlights that deployment decisions
   extend beyond initial placement.  Over time, functions may be
   updated, reconfigured, or extended to incorporate new models or new
   sensor modalities, and the same application logic may need to be
   deployed across multiple farms with different infrastructure
   configurations.  These lifecycle aspects underscore the relevance of
   an application-centric view that treats deployment as an ongoing
   process rather than a one-time event.

8.2.  Drone-Based Delivery in Urban Environments

   A drone-based delivery application uses unmanned aerial vehicles to
   deliver packages in urban areas.  The application comprises multiple
   functional components, including mission planning, navigation,
   sensing, local coordination, and backend services for order
   management and analytics.  From an application-centric perspective,
   these components form a logical application graph, with dependencies
   reflecting data flows and control interactions among functions.  The
   application operates across the full span of the IoT computing
   continuum, leveraging constrained onboard devices, nearby edge nodes,
   operator-provided cloudlets, and cloud platforms.

   Low-level sensing, actuation, and real-time navigation functions
   execute directly on the drone due to strict latency and control
   requirements.  Local coordination and situational awareness functions
   may execute on nearby edge nodes or gateways that aggregate
   information from multiple drones operating in the same area.
   Operator-provided cloudlets, for example, those associated with 5G or
   future 6G infrastructure, can host functions that support cooperative
   behavior among drones, such as collision avoidance assistance and
   shared situational awareness.  Backend services, including mission
   planning, optimization, logging, and analytics, typically run on
   cloud platforms that provide greater computational resources and
   global system visibility.

   This scenario is particularly relevant for illustrating the
   coexistence of different coordination styles within a single
   application.  Mission planning and high-level task assignment
   naturally follow an orchestration style, in which a centralized
   component coordinates delivery schedules and routes based on global
   system state.  In contrast, local interactions among drones, such as
   collision avoidance or cooperative sensing, follow a choreography
   style, relying on decentralized interactions and shared interaction
   rules without a single controlling entity.  Both coordination styles
   coexist within the same application and are influenced by deployment
   choices across the continuum, demonstrating that the distinction
   between orchestration and choreography is not merely theoretical but
   reflects concrete architectural trade-offs.

   Deployment decisions in this scenario are also inherently dynamic.
   As drones move through different environments or experience changing
   network conditions, mappings of coordination functions to edge nodes
   or cloudlets may need to be revised, while backend services remain
   centralized.  The application lifecycle extends beyond initial
   deployment, with components being updated, reconfigured, or migrated
   in response to changes in delivery demand, failures of individual
   drones, or variations in network availability.

8.3.  Discussion

   Together, these two scenarios illustrate the spectrum of smart
   applications that operate over the IoT computing continuum.  The
   first grounds the discussion in operational deployments with
   documented constraints, particularly around intermittent connectivity
   and the need for resilient edge behavior.  The second reveals the
   broader architectural space where placement, coordination, and
   lifecycle concerns fully manifest, including the explicit coexistence
   of orchestration and choreography within a single application.  Both
   share the fundamental property that application behavior emerges from
   the coordinated execution of distributed functions across
   heterogeneous stages, reinforcing the need for application-centric
   perspectives on deployment and lifecycle management.

9.  The Role of AI Agents in Application Deployment

   The previously discussed conceptual deployment model considers
   placement, coordination, and lifecycle management as interconnected
   and inherently dynamic concerns.  As the scale, heterogeneity, and
   dynamism of the Internet of Things (IoT) computing continuum
   increase, addressing these concerns through static policies or manual
   procedures becomes increasingly challenging, especially when
   application workloads demonstrate heterogeneous and context-sensitive
   behavior.  AI agents, defined as autonomous software entities capable
   of perceiving their environment, reasoning about objectives, and
   acting upon deployment infrastructure, present a promising solution
   for managing this complexity.  Foundational research on multi-agent
   systems [WOOLDRIDGE-AGENTS] offers established abstractions for
   understanding agent behavior.  This section analyzes the role of such
   agents from an application-centric perspective, without specifying
   particular agent architectures, frameworks, or protocols.

   AI agents are connected to application deployment in two
   complementary ways.  First, agents can facilitate the deployment
   process by assisting with or automating decisions regarding the
   placement, coordination, adaptation, and lifecycle management of
   application functions.  Second, agents themselves are software
   components that require execution within the continuum; therefore,
   their deployment must also be considered, subject to the same
   placement criteria as application functions.  The following
   discussion examines these two roles sequentially.

9.1.  Agents as Enablers of the Deployment Process

   In their first role, AI agents support deploying the services that
   comprise a smart application across the IoT computing continuum.
   Given a logical application graph and a set of available execution
   environments, agents can evaluate candidate mappings of functions to
   continuum stages, taking into account application-level requirements
   such as latency, resilience, energy consumption, and data locality,
   as well as runtime observations of infrastructure conditions.  Beyond
   initial placement, agents can drive lifecycle operations, including
   reconfiguration, scaling, migration, and partial redeployment, in
   response to workload variations, mobility, connectivity disruptions,
   or evolving physical contexts.

   An empirical example in [LLM-PLACEMENT] evaluates whole-request
   placement of the same Ollama-served qwen3.5:4b inference service for
   smart-irrigation contexts.  The local fog endpoint has four vCPUs and
   8 GB RAM, while the cloud endpoint uses an AWS g5.4xlarge instance
   with an NVIDIA A10G GPU.  This comparison characterizes the available
   heterogeneous endpoints; it does not isolate a location effect under
   equivalent hardware.

   A retrospective analysis covers 30 active cloud-available states
   formed from five agricultural contexts, three workload levels, and
   two fog conditions.  The cloud-first policy matches the lowest-
   latency successful endpoint in all 30 states.  A rule-based policy
   matches that retrospective oracle in 83.33% of states, with zero
   median regret but 29.194 s 95th-percentile regret, where regret is
   the selected endpoint's response time minus the oracle response time.
   These results do not establish an advantage for additional placement
   intelligence under the evaluated conditions.

   The endpoint campaigns report 154/154 completed cloud requests and
   495/532 completed requests across stable and degraded fog campaigns
   combined.  The fog measurements characterize the local execution path
   that can serve as a fallback; they do not establish guaranteed
   continuity or application-level correctness during a field outage.
   The campaigns have unequal sample sizes, and the paired-state
   analysis reuses each context-workload cloud measurement for both fog
   conditions.

   The prototype includes an LLM-based placement manager, but its
   complete cloud-available decision trace was not retained.  It is
   therefore excluded from the quantitative policy comparison.  The
   example motivates explicit availability constraints and simple
   baselines when evaluating agent-assisted placement, rather than a
   claim that an agent outperforms those baselines.  The study is an
   unpublished manuscript and its results are specific to the tested
   hardware, model, prompts, and workloads.

   Agent-assisted deployment does not presuppose a particular
   coordination style.  A single agent may coordinate deployment
   centrally, while multiple agents may exchange local observations and
   coordinate distributed actions.  Tool interaction protocols such as
   [MCP] and agent interaction protocols such as [A2A] are relevant
   examples, but neither protocol determines whether an application uses
   orchestration or choreography.  The deployment problem includes
   deciding which observations are available locally, which actions an
   agent may authorize, and what behavior remains available if the agent
   or its communication path fails.

9.2.  Deployment of the Agents Themselves

   In their secondary role, agents function as deployable entities.  As
   software components, they require execution environments that provide
   sufficient computational power, memory, and connectivity.  Agents may
   operate at any location within the IoT computing continuum, including
   gateways, edge nodes, cloudlets, and cloud platforms.  The selected
   deployment stage influences the timeliness of agent decisions, the
   degree of local context awareness, and resilience to connectivity
   disruptions.

   Constrained devices necessitate special consideration, as many do not
   possess sufficient resources to support a complete agent runtime.
   Nevertheless, deployment strategies must often account for device-
   level conditions.  A proxy-based approach addresses this challenge: a
   lightweight proxy on the constrained device represents it to an agent
   located at a proximate stage, such as a gateway or edge node, which
   then performs reasoning on behalf of the device.  This is a candidate
   pattern, not an assumption that every constrained device can host a
   proxy.  Its feasibility depends on the device resource class, the
   communication budget, and the freshness and authority of the
   information exposed.  Representation may instead need to reside at a
   gateway.

   Agent deployment exhibits a recursive nature, as the mechanisms used
   by agents to deploy application functions are equally applicable to
   the agents themselves.  The placement, migration, and lifecycle
   management of agents present challenges analogous to those
   encountered with application components, but also introduce
   additional concerns regarding trust, accountability, and control,
   particularly when agents operate autonomously across multiple
   administrative domains.  The Research Directions section identifies
   open questions associated with this dual role.

10.  Relationship to Ongoing Research and Standardization

10.1.  Edge Functions and Application Deployment

   [RFC9556] describes IoT edge challenges and functions.  This document
   complements that perspective by examining application dependencies
   and lifecycle decisions across the environments that host those
   functions.  The distinction is one of emphasis: infrastructure and
   application concerns overlap, and the intent is not to assign them to
   disjoint layers.

   Research on the IoT computing continuum [IOTINUUM] and deployment-
   aware approaches such as IoTDeploy [IOTDEPLOY] provides examples of
   application decomposition and placement.  [CONTINUUM-SURVEY] gives
   broader context.  These works inform the problem space without
   prescribing the abstractions used here.  Community discussions such
   as [WPIETF2025] also motivate engagement with the IETF and IRTF.

10.2.  Device Update, Discovery, and Management

   The SUIT firmware update architecture [RFC9019] addresses secure
   firmware update for IoT devices.  It provides lifecycle foundations
   for device software, while application deployment raises additional
   questions about compatibility and coordinated transitions across
   device firmware, edge services, and cloud functions.

   CoRE work provides complementary mechanisms.  The Resource Directory
   [RFC9176] supports resource registration and lookup, including for
   endpoints that cannot remain continuously available.  The CoAP
   Management Interface [CORE-COMI], an expired work-in-progress draft,
   is relevant to accessing management data in constrained environments.
   Resource discovery and management can supply inputs and operations
   for deployment, but do not by themselves describe an application's
   entire dependency graph or determine a safe multi-component
   transition.  Investigating how existing mechanisms can be composed is
   preferable to assuming that a new management interface is needed.

10.3.  Computing, Traffic Steering, and Intent

   Work in COINRG [COINRG] explored computation within networks and the
   relationship between compute and networking resources.  Its research
   provides context for the execution environments considered here.
   This document concentrates on application behavior across those
   environments, including dependencies on physical devices.

   The CATS charter [CATS] concerns traffic steering using computing and
   network information.  Choosing among service instances and deciding
   where to instantiate or migrate an application function are related
   but distinct decisions.  Traffic steering can use existing instances;
   application deployment also considers function dependencies,
   persistent state, and lifecycle transitions.  This document does not
   assume that CATS specifies application placement.

   NMRG's work on intent-based networking, including the concepts and
   definitions in [RFC9315], is relevant to expressing desired outcomes
   separately from operational actions.  A research question here is how
   application objectives, such as retaining local behavior during
   disconnection, can be translated into deployment constraints and
   checked against observed behavior.  Such a mapping is not specified
   by this document.

10.4.  Descriptions of Things and Application Abstractions

   The Semantic Definition Format (SDF) [RFC9880] describes data and
   interaction models of Things.  W3C WoT Thing Descriptions [WOT-TD]
   describe Things and their interaction affordances, including
   information used to access those interactions.  Both are candidates
   for reuse when describing the device-facing capabilities on which
   application functions depend.

   Neither is treated here as a complete deployment language.  Open
   questions include how such descriptions can be related to function
   dependencies, runtime requirements, permissible placements, state
   migration, and degraded operation.  The appropriate reuse,
   composition, or extension of existing descriptions requires
   investigation before proposing new application-centric abstractions.

11.  Research Questions

   The preceding sections identify the concepts and constraints.  The
   following questions focus on unresolved issues that could guide a
   shared application-layer problem statement for discussion in T2TRG.
   They do not imply research-group adoption or consensus.

   *  Description and reuse: Which deployment-relevant information can
      be obtained from SDF, WoT Thing Descriptions, and existing
      management mechanisms?  What additional information is needed to
      express application dependencies, physical anchoring, and
      permissible degraded behavior?

   *  Continuity and reconciliation: How can an application specify
      behavior available during disconnection, the validity of cached
      observations and commands, and the reconciliation of state and
      control authority after reconnection?

   *  Lifecycle consistency: How can compatibility and safe recovery be
      assessed across independently updated device, edge, and cloud
      components when atomic rollout is infeasible?

   *  Placement and coordination: Which dependencies limit movement of
      functions, and how can a change of placement or coordination style
      preserve application-level behavior across administrative
      boundaries?

   *  Agent responsibility and control: Which deployment actions may an
      agent perform, how are those actions audited, and how are the
      agent and any device proxies themselves updated, recovered, or
      replaced without losing essential local control?

   *  Evaluation: Which workloads and failure conditions expose
      meaningful trade-offs beyond a simple baseline?  Evaluations need
      to distinguish endpoint capacity, connectivity, policy decision
      cost, service completion, and correctness of physical behavior.
      Complete decision traces are needed to attribute outcomes to an
      agent.

   A useful next step is to determine whether these questions form a
   coherent application-layer problem statement complementary to
   [RFC9556], and which can be addressed by composing existing work.
   Further scenarios and deployment evidence can help establish that
   scope before mechanisms or new interfaces are proposed.

12.  Security Considerations

   This document does not specify a protocol.  Application deployment
   nevertheless affects security because it changes where code and data
   reside, which identities may act, and which components can influence
   physical behavior.

   In the IoT computing continuum, application components are
   distributed across heterogeneous and potentially untrusted execution
   environments, increasing the attack surface and complicating
   traditional security assumptions.  Deployment decisions influence
   trust boundaries, data exposure, and the ability to isolate faults or
   compromised components.

   Coordination strategies also have security implications.
   Orchestration-based approaches may introduce centralized control
   points that become attractive attack targets, while choreography-
   based approaches rely on decentralized interactions that require
   robust authentication, authorization, and trust management among
   components.

   The tight coupling between development and deployment further
   emphasizes the need to consider security throughout the application
   lifecycle.  Design-time assumptions about trust, isolation, and
   access control may be invalidated by deployment changes, migration of
   components, or dynamic adaptation at runtime.  As a result, security
   considerations must be revisited whenever application structure,
   placement, or coordination strategies evolve.

   Finally, the distributed and multi-domain nature of the IoT computing
   continuum raises challenges related to identity management, secure
   communication, and policy enforcement across administrative
   boundaries.  While addressing these challenges is outside the scope
   of this document, they underscore the importance of integrating
   security considerations into research and standardization efforts
   related to application-centric deployment and lifecycle management.

   Deployment agents and device proxies require particular care.
   Observations and proposed actions can be stale, forged, or outside
   the authority of the reporting component.  A proxy needs an explicit
   authorization scope; representing a device does not grant
   unrestricted authority to operate it.  Deployment changes also need
   to account for command replay, conflicting controllers, state
   disclosure during migration, and recovery after a partial update.
   Local safeguards and operator control remain relevant even when a
   deployment decision is automated.
